Apps, textbooks and videos can teach you a lot about a language, but at some point you need to use it with a real person. Chatting is one of the most effective ways to do that, because it combines reading, writing and real communication in a low-pressure setting. You can take your time, look up words and learn from every mistake. Here are ten practical ways to make the most of it.

## 1. Start with a goal

Before you begin, decide what you want from your practice. It might be holding a five-minute chat, writing without a dictionary or understanding native slang. A clear and small goal keeps you motivated and lets you measure progress. "This week I will have three conversations in Spanish" is better than "I want to be fluent."

## 2. Begin with greetings and simple questions

Do not start with a speech. Greet the person in their language, introduce yourself and ask one easy question. The words you already know are enough to begin, and each short exchange builds confidence. If you need ideas, our list of twenty ways to say hello in different languages is a good place to find a friendly opening.

## 3. Be honest that you are learning

Tell your partner that you are practising. Most people react with kindness and patience, and many will slow down, simplify their sentences or offer corrections. A message such as "I am learning Portuguese, so please forgive my mistakes" sets a relaxed tone and takes pressure off you.

## 4. Write first, then check

When you want to say something, try to build the sentence yourself before you use a translator. Then check it. This habit forces your brain to work, which is where learning happens. Use translation as a safety net, not a crutch. If you always translate first, you will never find out what you can already do.

## 5. Ask for gentle corrections

Mistakes are useful only if you notice them. Ask your partner to point out errors, but only the important ones, so you do not feel overwhelmed. "Could you tell me when something sounds strange?" works well. When someone corrects you, thank them and write the correct version down. Our article on how to say thank you in twenty languages gives you a few ways to do that in someone else's language.

## 6. Keep a personal phrase notebook

Every chat gives you new words and phrases. Keep a simple notebook or note on your phone and add the ones you want to remember. Write the phrase, its meaning and a short example from your conversation. Review the list a few times a week. Words you have actually used in a real conversation tend to stick far better than words from a list.

## 7. Mix text and voice

Text chat gives you time to think, while voice notes train your ear and your pronunciation. A good pattern is to start with text and add voice once you feel comfortable. Listen to your partner's voice notes more than once: the first time for general meaning, the second for details. Recording your own voice and listening back is awkward at first and extremely useful.

## 8. Talk about what you love

You will learn faster when the topic interests you. Ask your partner about music, football, cooking, games, films or anything else you like. You will pick up the vocabulary that matters to you, and the conversation will feel natural rather than like homework. Shared interests are also the quickest route to real friendships.

## 9. Learn the informal side

Textbooks teach correct language, but real chats are full of abbreviations, slang and short forms. Ask your partner how people really write to each other. Learn how they laugh in text, as we explain in our piece on how the world laughs in text, and how they shorten common phrases. Be careful with slang you do not fully understand, and ask before using it, because some expressions are only suitable for close friends. Also watch out for false friends, the words that look familiar but mean something different, which we cover in a separate article.

## 10. Be consistent, not perfect

Ten minutes a day beats two hours once a month. Regular short practice keeps the language fresh in your mind and builds a habit. If you miss a day, do not worry about it. Simply pick up the next conversation. After a few weeks, look back at your first messages and compare them to your latest ones. Seeing the progress is one of the best motivators there is.

## Practical tips for finding partners

You can find people to practise with in language exchange communities, in online groups and in chat apps that connect you with people around the world. Look for someone who also wants to learn your language, because that creates a fair trade: you help them and they help you. Be clear about how you would like to split the time, for example half the chat in each language.

Protect your privacy as you would anywhere online. Do not share personal details too early, ignore anyone who asks for money, and block or report people who make you uncomfortable. A good language partner respects your boundaries.

## Make it a habit

The secret of learning a language by chatting is not talent. It is repetition, curiosity and a willingness to look a bit silly. Every sentence you write is a small step, and every reply you understand is proof that the effort works. Start with one message today. It does not have to be perfect, only real.
