Politeness is universal, but the rules are not. Everyone wants to be treated with respect, yet what counts as respectful changes from one culture to the next. Some societies value direct honesty, others value harmony and indirectness. The same sentence can sound refreshing in one place and rude in another. If you talk to people from different countries, understanding these differences will save you from many misunderstandings.

## Direct and indirect communication

One of the biggest differences is how directly people say what they mean. In countries such as Germany, the Netherlands and Israel, people usually say exactly what they think, and honest feedback is a sign of respect. Saying "I do not agree" or "This does not work" is not seen as an attack.

In many parts of Asia, Latin America and the Middle East, people often prefer a gentler, more indirect style. The aim is to protect everyone's dignity and keep the relationship smooth. A phrase that sounds like agreement may actually mean "I have heard you" and not "I will do it." Anthropologists often describe this as the difference between low-context and high-context communication: in the first, the words carry the meaning, and in the second, much of the meaning sits in the situation and in what is not said.

Neither style is better. The key is to avoid judging someone by your own standards. A direct person is not necessarily rude, and an indirect person is not necessarily dishonest.

## How to say no

Refusing politely is a skill that varies a great deal. In Japan, a plain "no" is often avoided. Instead, people say things like "that would be difficult" or "I will think about it", and listeners understand that this is a refusal. In China, similar softer phrasing is common, particularly in formal situations. In contrast, in the Netherlands or Germany, a direct "no, thank you" is completely normal and is not taken personally.

When you are the one refusing, you can combine both approaches: be clear but kind. "Thank you so much for the invitation. I cannot make it this time, but I really appreciate you thinking of me." When you receive a vague answer from someone, do not push. Ask a gentle question such as "Would another day be better?" which gives them an easy way out.

## Giving and receiving compliments

Compliments follow different scripts too. In the United States, people usually accept a compliment with a simple "thank you" and may add one back. In many East Asian cultures, it is customary to play down praise: if you tell someone their English is excellent, they may reply that it is not good at all. This is modesty, not a rejection of your kindness.

In some places, openly admiring someone's possessions can create an expectation that they will offer it to you. This is sometimes mentioned in connection with hospitality traditions in parts of the Middle East and South Asia. It is not a universal rule, but it is a reminder that a simple remark can mean more than you intended.

If you are unsure, compliment effort, skill or taste rather than money or appearance. "You explain things really clearly" is safe almost everywhere.

## Ritual politeness and offers

Some cultures have formal systems of polite offers and refusals. The best known example is [[taarof]] in Iran, a form of etiquette in which people offer things generously and may refuse offers several times before accepting. A host might insist that a guest eat more, and the guest might politely decline before finally agreeing. Both sides understand the game.

Similar patterns exist elsewhere, such as in China, where guests may refuse a gift or a seat at the table at first to show humility. If you do not know the custom, the safest approach is to offer more than once and to accept graciously when someone insists.

## Please, thank you and sorry

Words like "please", "thank you" and "sorry" are used with different frequency. British English uses "sorry" and "please" constantly, even for small things. Some languages use them far less in everyday speech, and people there may find constant thanks slightly formal or distant. In Japan, the word [[sumimasen]] works as an apology, a thank-you and a way to get attention, all in one.

Our article on how to say thank you in twenty languages shows how widely the expressions vary. As a rule, a sincere thank you is welcome in every culture, and it is rarely wrong to say it.

## Formality and titles

Forms of address are another area where politeness shows. In many languages, you choose between a formal and an informal "you", and using the wrong one can seem either cold or overfamiliar. We explain this in our article on why many languages have two words for "you". In some cultures, using first names right away is friendly. In others, it is expected to use a title and last name until invited to do otherwise.

When you are not sure, start formal. It is easier to relax later than to repair a bad first impression.

## Politeness in online chat

Online, all these differences remain, and they are harder to spot because there is no face or voice. A few habits help. Keep your messages warm and clear, avoid sarcasm, and do not take short answers personally. If something sounds rude, give it the benefit of the doubt. The other person may be translating, typing quickly or following another set of rules.

Ask when you are unsure. "Is it okay if I ask about your family?" or "Please tell me if I say something that sounds strange in your culture" shows humility, and almost everyone responds well to it.

## What to remember

Politeness is a way of showing that you care about the other person. The words and gestures change, but the intention stays the same. If you are curious, patient and willing to adapt, you will rarely go far wrong, and people will forgive the occasional mistake because they can see what you mean.
