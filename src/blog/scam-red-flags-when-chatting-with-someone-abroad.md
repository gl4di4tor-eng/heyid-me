Meeting people from other countries is one of the best things about chatting online. It is also something scammers take advantage of. They count on the fact that you cannot easily check who someone is, where they live or whether their story is true.

The good news is that most scams follow a handful of patterns. Once you know them, they become easy to spot. This guide covers the ones to watch for and what to do if you meet one.

## First, a fair warning in the other direction

Being from another country is not a red flag. The vast majority of people you meet in a global chat are just curious, friendly and looking for a conversation, the same as you. Scammers exist in every country, and judging people by where they come from is both unfair and unhelpful. Watch for behavior, not nationality.

## Why these scams work

Most scams are slow. The person writes every day, pays you generous compliments and becomes part of your routine. Only after weeks of that does a "crisis" appear, and by then you care about them. This is why smart, careful people fall for it. The scam does not target your intelligence. It targets your kindness and your wish to connect. Knowing that, you can enjoy new friendships and still keep your guard up on the one topic that matters.

## The most common red flags

**1. Money comes up.** This is the big one. A person who asks for money, gift cards, crypto, a "small loan" or help paying for something is a major red flag, however good the reason sounds and however long you have been talking. A real friendship does not need a transfer from someone you have never met.

**2. Declarations of love arrive very fast.** If someone says they are in love with you after a few days, or calls you their soulmate before you have spoken properly, slow down. Scammers use intense affection to build trust quickly.

**3. They always have a reason not to video chat.** A broken camera, a bad connection, a remote location. One excuse is fine, but a pattern of avoiding a live call is a warning sign.

**4. The story is very dramatic.** Stranded abroad, a sick relative, a stolen wallet, a legal problem, a military or oil-rig job in a faraway place. Real life has emergencies, but scammers' emergencies always seem to need your money.

**5. They push you off the app.** Early requests to move to a private messenger, or to click a link they send, are worth treating carefully. Platforms have reporting tools. Private chats do not.

**6. There is urgency.** "I need it today." "There is no time to explain." Pressure is designed to stop you from thinking.

**7. They ask for sensitive details.** Passport photos, bank details, one-time codes, passwords or your home address. Never share these with someone you have just met.

**8. They offer investments or "opportunities."** A new friend who suddenly wants to teach you about trading or a guaranteed profit is almost always selling something that costs you money.

**9. Their photos and details do not line up.** Perfect pictures, a profile that is hard to match with their story, or details that change from one conversation to the next.

## A quick self-check

Ask yourself these four questions. If you answer yes to any of them, pause the conversation.

- Has this person asked for money, directly or indirectly?
- Do I feel rushed or pressured?
- Has anything about their story changed or stopped adding up?
- Am I being asked to keep our conversation secret?

## What to do if something feels off

1. **Do not send money or personal documents.** Not even a small amount, not even as a test.
2. **Ask for a live video call.** A real person can usually do it. If they avoid it repeatedly, that tells you something.
3. **Run a reverse image search on their photos.** Copy their picture into a reverse image tool and see whether it belongs to someone else.
4. **Keep the conversation on the platform.** That way you can report it.
5. **Block and report.** You do not owe anyone an explanation. Use the block and report options in the app.
6. **Tell someone you trust.** Scammers rely on you keeping quiet. Saying it out loud to a friend breaks the spell.

## If you already sent money

First, do not blame yourself. These scams are designed by professionals and catch smart people every day. Then act quickly: contact your bank or payment provider and explain what happened, because some transfers can be stopped or investigated if you report fast. Keep screenshots of the conversation. Report it to the platform, and to your local police or consumer protection agency, which usually have a way to report online fraud.

## A note for younger users

If you are a teenager, the same rules apply, and a few more. Never share your address, school, photos you would not want public or any private information, and tell a parent or another trusted adult if someone makes you uncomfortable or asks you to keep secrets. You are never in trouble for reporting something.
