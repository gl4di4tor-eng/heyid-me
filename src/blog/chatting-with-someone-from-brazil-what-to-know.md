If you have never talked to someone from Brazil, the first chat may surprise you. It tends to warm up fast. Messages arrive with emoji, voice notes, nicknames and a lot of laughter, and within ten minutes you can feel like you have known the person for a while.

Here is what helps: the one language mistake to avoid, how the friendly tone works, what people enjoy talking about and a few things worth leaving for later.

## First, the language: it's Portuguese

Brazil is the biggest country in South America and the largest Portuguese-speaking country in the world. That means Brazilians speak Portuguese, not Spanish. It sounds obvious, but it is the most common slip, and opening with Spanish can feel like being mixed up with the neighbors.

Portuguese and Spanish are close relatives, so many people can follow a bit of each other's written language. Still, if you are writing in a language that is not English, make it Portuguese. A simple [[oi]] (pronounced "oy") is the everyday hello, and [[obrigado]] or [[obrigada]] means thank you. Men say the first and women say the second, matching their own gender. Our [20 ways to say hello](/blog/20-ways-to-say-hello-in-different-languages/) and [say hello tool](/discover/say-hello/) have the pronunciation.

## Expect warmth, and match it

Brazilian chats are often informal very quickly. People use first names or nicknames, add diminutives to make things sound friendlier, and sign off with [[abraço]] (a hug) or [[beijo]] (a kiss). In most cases that is just friendliness and does not mean anything romantic, so you do not need to read it as flirting.

You may also get a lot of voice messages. Voice notes are a normal way to chat in Brazil, and a long one is not a hint that something is wrong. If you would rather reply in text, that is fine, and most people will not mind.

When something is funny, you will see "kkkk" instead of "haha". It is the sound of laughter written out. If you want the full story, we explained it in [how the world laughs in text](/blog/how-the-world-laughs-in-text/).

## Topics that tend to go well

- **Where they live.** Brazil is enormous, and a person from Rio, São Paulo, Salvador, Porto Alegre or a small inland town will describe very different lives. "What is your city like?" gets better answers than "What is Brazil like?"
- **Food.** Think feijoada, pão de queijo, churrasco, açaí. Ask what they actually cook at home, or what a visitor should eat first in their region.
- **Music.** Samba and bossa nova are famous, but people also listen to funk, sertanejo, pagode and a lot of international pop. Ask what they have been playing lately.
- **Football.** Many Brazilians have an opinion about the game, so ask which club they support. A little teasing is usually fine, as long as it stays friendly.
- **Holidays and traditions.** Carnival is the best-known one, but Brazil also has Festa Junina in June, with its food and dances. Ask which celebration they look forward to most.

## Small things to avoid

**Assuming Spanish.** We covered it, but it is worth saying twice.

**Reducing Brazil to Carnival, beaches and football.** Those are real parts of the country, but most people also have jobs, studies, families and ordinary Tuesdays. Asking about their day is better than asking them to represent a postcard.

**Opening with crime or poverty.** Some people ask "isn't Brazil dangerous?" as an icebreaker. It rarely lands well. If the topic comes up, let the other person lead.

**Jumping into politics.** As in most places, strong opinions are easier to share once you know each other.

**The OK hand sign.** The thumb and finger circle can be taken as rude in Brazil. Our guide to [gestures that mean different things](/blog/gestures-that-mean-different-things-around-the-world/) explains it, and it is a good reason to prefer words over the 👌 emoji.

## A few words you may see

Brazilian chats are full of short, friendly expressions. You will often meet [[valeu]] (thanks, cheers), [[beleza]] (cool, all good) and [[tá bom]] (okay). You may also hear [[saudade]], a word for a gentle longing that has no single English equivalent. We wrote about it in [words with no English translation](/blog/words-with-no-english-translation/).

The word for "you" is also worth knowing. [[Você]] is the standard form across most of Brazil, while [[tu]] is common in some regions. If someone uses one with you, you can simply follow their lead.

## Time zones

Brazil has several time zones, but most people live in Brasília time, which is UTC−3. The country stopped using daylight saving time in 2019, so the gap with you changes only when your own clock changes. ## Plans, time and flexibility

In social situations, many Brazilians treat times as flexible. A relaxed attitude to being a bit late is common among friends. That is a generalization and it varies by city, age and context, so see [attitudes to time around the world](/blog/is-it-rude-to-be-late-attitudes-to-time-around-the-world/) before you decide someone is unreliable. A slow reply may just mean life got in the way.

## Five openers to borrow

1. Which city are you in, and what is it like there?
2. What is a dish from your region that I should try?
3. What music have you been listening to this week?
4. Which football club do you support, or are you not into it?
5. How do people in your town celebrate Festa Junina?

For more ideas, try the [50 questions to ask someone from another country](/blog/50-questions-to-ask-someone-from-another-country/).

## Last thought

Brazilian conversations reward warmth. Write like you are happy to be there, ask a real question and answer theirs in return. The rest tends to take care of itself.
