You matched with someone from Japan, and now you are wondering how to begin. Good news: you do not need to know much. A little curiosity and a few habits will take you a long way.

This guide covers the ones that matter most: how politeness shows up in messages, what people tend to enjoy talking about, and how to read a reply that seems indirect.

## A quick note before we start

Japan has more than 120 million people, and the person you are talking to might be a teenager in Osaka, a nurse in Sapporo or a retired teacher in a small town. Everything below describes tendencies, not rules. If the person in front of you behaves differently, trust them over any article, this one included.

## Start polite, then relax

Japanese has built-in levels of politeness, and many people carry that habit into English. Their first messages may be careful, a little formal and softened with phrases like "nice to meet you" or "thank you for talking with me."

You do not have to copy that. Just be warm and a bit more gentle than you would be with a close friend. Skip pushing for personal details in the first few messages, and let the other person set the pace.

You will usually notice when things loosen up. Messages get shorter, emoji appear, laughter shows up as "(笑)" or a row of "w". That is your cue to relax too. Our guide to [how the world laughs in text](/blog/how-the-world-laughs-in-text/) explains those signs.

If you are unsure how to address them, just ask: "What would you like me to call you?" It is polite in any country.

## Topics that usually go well

These are safe, friendly and easy to answer in a second language.

- **Food.** What did they eat today? What is the specialty of their hometown? Food is a universal opener, and many people enjoy explaining regional dishes.
- **Seasons and weather.** Seasons matter a lot in everyday Japanese life, from cherry blossoms in spring to the rainy season and hot summers. Asking what the season is like right now feels natural.
- **Their hometown.** Ask about where they live, not just Tokyo or Kyoto. Smaller places often have the best stories.
- **Hobbies.** Music, games, sports, books, cooking, cycling. Ask what they have been into lately.
- **Language.** If they are studying English, they may enjoy helping you with a few Japanese words. A simple "[[konnichiwa]]" goes a long way, and our [say hello tool](/discover/say-hello/) has it with pronunciation.

Anime and manga are popular in Japan, but do not assume everyone loves them. Ask, do not presume. "Do you watch anything you'd recommend?" works better than "So you must like anime, right?"

## Reading between the lines

Japanese communication is often described as indirect, and you may notice it in chat. A few patterns are worth knowing.

A "maybe" or "that might be a little difficult" can be a gentle way of saying no. A soft refusal is not an invitation to push harder.

"[[Hai]]" does not always mean "I agree." It can simply mean "I hear you." If you ask a yes or no question about something important, check again in a different way.

Slow replies are not rejection. People are busy, time zones differ and some people simply write less. If you offer options ("Would you rather talk about food or music?"), you make it easier to answer without feeling pressured.

You may also see short responses such as "I see" or "that's interesting" appear often while you are telling a story. In Japanese conversation this kind of reply, called [[aizuchi]], mostly means "I'm following you." It is a sign of attention, not boredom, so keep going.

Because nuance is easy to lose in translation, read [why a translated message can sound rude](/blog/why-your-translated-message-sounds-rude/) before you decide a short reply means anything negative.

## Small mistakes worth avoiding

- **Going deep too fast.** Questions about relationships, money or family problems in the first conversation can feel intrusive. Build a bit of trust first.
- **Heavy topics at the start.** Politics and history are real conversations, but they are easier once you know each other.
- **Heavy slang and idioms.** They are hard to translate. Plain sentences travel better. See [idioms that don't translate](/blog/idioms-that-dont-translate/) for examples.
- **Expecting instant replies.** Japan is usually nine hours ahead of UTC and does not use daylight saving time, so your afternoon might be their night.

## Mind the clock

Japan Standard Time is UTC+9 all year. If you live in Europe, their evening is your late morning or afternoon. If you live in the US, their evening is your early morning, and the gap changes with daylight saving. Our guide to the [best time to chat across continents](/blog/best-time-to-chat-with-someone-on-another-continent/) has real examples, and the [world clock](/discover/world-now/) shows what time it is in Tokyo right now.

## Five openers you can borrow

1. What is the season like where you are right now?
2. What is a dish from your hometown that visitors should try?
3. What have you been listening to or playing lately?
4. What is a small place near you that you love?
5. Is there a Japanese word you think I should learn first?

## The one thing to remember

Curiosity beats knowledge. Nobody expects you to understand an entire country. They just want to feel that you are interested in them and not in a stereotype. Ask a real question, listen to the answer and ask a follow-up. That is all it takes in Japanese, in English and everywhere else.
