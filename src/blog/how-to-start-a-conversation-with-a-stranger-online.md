Writing to a stranger online feels risky because you have almost nothing to go on: no face, no tone of voice, no friend in common to break the ice. The good news is that a good first message is a skill, and a simple one. This guide covers what to say, what to skip and how to make the first minute comfortable for both of you.

## Why a bare "hi" rarely works

A single "hi" puts all the work on the other person. They have to decide whether to answer, guess what you want and invent the next line themselves. Plenty of people simply do not bother. A better first message does three small jobs at once: it greets, it shows that you are a real person with a real interest, and it makes replying easy.

Compare "hi" with "Hi! I saw that you like cooking. What is the one dish you could eat every week?" The second message costs you ten extra seconds, but it gives the other person something specific to answer. It also shows that you paid attention, which is the quickest way to stop feeling like a stranger.

## Use something you actually know

Look for context before you type. On a chat app that matches you with random people, the context may be only a country or a language. That is still enough. Greeting someone in their own language, even with a single word such as [[hola]] or [[ciao]], usually earns a smile and a faster reply.

If you know nothing at all, ask about something universal: what time it is where they are, what they are doing today, what the weather is like. These are not exciting questions, but they are easy to answer and they open the door to better topics.

## Three openers that work

The first is the curious opener: a short, genuine question about their country, language or day. "What is a normal Saturday like where you live?" works because everybody has an answer and nobody has to be an expert.

The second is the shared-moment opener. If it is morning for you and night for them, say so. "It is just after breakfast here. What time is it for you?" Time differences are a natural talking point, and they remind both of you that you are reaching across the world, which is the whole point of talking to someone abroad.

The third is the small-confession opener: "I am learning a little Spanish and I am nervous about mistakes. Can I practise with you?" People tend to respond warmly to honesty, and it sets a relaxed tone where mistakes are fine.

## If you are writing in a second language

Many conversations with people from other countries happen in a language that is not native to one or both of you. Keep your sentences short and your words simple. Avoid slang and jokes that depend on wordplay, because they rarely survive the trip. One idea per message is easier to read than a long paragraph, and it makes translation tools more accurate too.

Be patient with the other person's English or whatever language you share. If a reply is short or oddly phrased, it is usually a matter of effort, not attitude. A friendly "no problem, take your time" goes a long way.

## What to avoid in the first minute

Skip questions that feel like an interview: age, job, salary, relationship status and exactly where someone lives. In some cultures these are ordinary small talk, but with a stranger online they can feel intrusive, and the other person has no reason yet to trust you. Skip compliments about appearance as well. They are easy to misread and push the chat in a direction the other person may not want.

Do not paste the same message to everyone. People can tell, and it kills the feeling of a real conversation. Keep a few favourite openers, but change a detail each time.

Finally, leave heavy topics such as politics and religion for later. They are not forbidden, but they are much easier to discuss once there is some goodwill between you.

## Keep your own safety in mind

Most people you meet online are friendly, but it costs nothing to be careful. Do not share your full name, address, school or workplace, phone number or private photos in the first conversation. Be wary of anyone who asks for money, pushes you to move to another app straight away, or becomes angry when you decline a question. Those are common warning signs.

On any decent chat app you can block or report someone and walk away. Using those tools is not rude. They exist for exactly this reason, and you never owe a stranger a long conversation.

## When the message lands and when it does not

Sometimes you will write a thoughtful first message and receive nothing, or a single word. That is normal. The other person may be busy, asleep, shy or tired of using a second language. Do not take it personally and do not send a string of follow-ups. Send one message, give it time and move on if nothing comes.

When someone does answer, answer their answer. Pick one detail from their reply and ask about it. This habit of following up is the biggest difference between a chat that dies in a minute and one that lasts an hour.

## A simple pattern to remember

Greet them, say something specific, ask one easy question. That is all. "Hi! Good morning from here. I noticed you are in Brazil. What is the best thing to eat there?" Short, warm and easy to answer.
