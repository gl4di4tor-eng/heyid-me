Texting looks universal. You type, you press send, and the other person reads it. But the unwritten rules around texting vary more than most people expect. How fast you are supposed to reply, whether a voice note is friendly or annoying, and what an emoji means can all depend on where someone grew up. If you chat with people from other countries, knowing these differences can prevent a lot of unnecessary confusion.

## How fast should you reply?

In some places, a quick reply is a sign of interest and respect. In others, people expect to answer when they have time, and a delay of several hours is perfectly normal. Neither is rude, but they can clash. If you reply within a minute and the other person takes a day, you might feel ignored. If you take a day and they reply instantly, they might feel pressured.

Time zones add another layer. Someone who does not answer for eight hours may simply be asleep. Before you decide that a quiet chat means disinterest, check what time it is for them. Our guide to the best time to chat with someone on another continent can help with that.

A good habit is to avoid reading too much into timing. If a reply matters, a friendly "no rush, answer when you can" removes the pressure for both sides.

## Short messages and long messages

Message length is another quiet signal. Some people write one long message with everything in it. Others send ten short ones in a row. Neither is wrong, but they feel different. A very short reply such as "ok" is neutral to some people and cold to others, especially if the previous messages were warm.

When you are not sure how to read a short answer, assume the best. The person may be on a phone, writing in a second language, or simply busy. If you want more, ask an open question, which invites a longer answer.

## Voice notes: friendly or intrusive?

Voice messages are extremely popular in many places, including large parts of Latin America, the Middle East, Africa and China, where people often send them instead of typing. They feel personal and quick. In other places, people consider them inconvenient, because they cannot be skimmed and are awkward to play in public.

If you are chatting in a second language, voice notes have another effect: accents and speed can make them harder to understand than text. A good rule is to ask first. "Do you prefer text or voice messages?" shows consideration and avoids frustration.

## Emojis do not mean the same thing everywhere

Emojis seem to be a universal language, yet they are interpreted differently across cultures and age groups. The thumbs-up is friendly to many people but can feel curt or even rude to some, as we discuss in our article on gestures that mean different things around the world. The folded-hands emoji means "thank you" or "please" to many people in Asia, but it is often read as a prayer or a high five elsewhere.

Even the way people laugh in text differs. English speakers write "haha", Spanish speakers "jajaja", Thai speakers "555" and Brazilians "kkkk". Our piece on how the world laughs in text explains why. If you use a laughing emoji, be aware that it may be considered childish by some groups and perfectly normal by others.

The safest approach is to use emojis in moderation, choose simple ones such as a smile, and copy the style of the person you are talking to.

## Punctuation and tone

In English, a full stop at the end of a short message can feel cold or even angry, especially among younger people. Capital letters can feel like shouting. In other languages, these signals are different or absent. A person who always uses formal punctuation may be doing so out of politeness, not irritation.

Exclamation marks are a good example. They often add warmth in English, such as "Thanks!" or "Great to meet you!", but in some cultures too many of them feel exaggerated. When you are talking to someone for the first time, a couple of friendly exclamation marks are fine. Over time, adapt to how they write.

## Formality in messages

Many languages have formal and informal ways of addressing someone, and these carry over into text. If you write in Spanish, German, French or Polish, using the informal "you" with someone older or someone you have just met may sound too familiar. If you are not sure, begin formally. Our article on formal and informal "you" explains how to choose.

Titles and names matter as well. Some cultures prefer to be addressed with a title or a polite form, while others find that stiff. Pay attention to how the other person introduces themselves and follow that.

## What about read receipts and "seen"?

Read receipts add stress for many people. Seeing "seen" with no answer can feel like a rejection, even though the other person might have read the message while walking and planned to answer later. Some people turn the feature off for exactly that reason. Do not take silence personally, and avoid sending several follow-ups to "check" whether they saw it.

## A short checklist

Before you worry about a message, run through these questions. What time is it for them? Could they be writing in a second language? Is this a cultural difference in style? Would a friendly question clear things up? Most texting misunderstandings are not about the person. They are about the missing context that texting leaves out.

Once you start thinking this way, you will see that online conversations become much more relaxed. You stop reading hidden meaning into everything, and you start enjoying what is actually there: a person on the other side of the world who wants to talk to you.
