Somewhere, a person is typing a message to you in their second language. They have probably rewritten it three times. They are hoping it makes sense, and they are quietly worried that you will think less of them if it does not.

How you reply matters more than you might expect. A good partner can make someone feel brave. A careless one can make them stop writing. Here is how to be the first kind.

## Write clearly, not childishly

The most helpful thing you can do is write in a way that is easy to follow, without turning into baby talk. There is a difference.

- **Use short, complete sentences.** Not "Me go store, you come?" but "I'm going to the store. Do you want to come?"
- **Keep your grammar correct.** Broken grammar does not help a learner. It teaches them mistakes.
- **Avoid slang, idioms and sarcasm.** "I'm gonna grab a bite, wanna come?" is hard to decode. "I'm going to eat something. Do you want to come?" is easy. See our guide to [idioms that don't translate](/blog/idioms-that-dont-translate/) for what trips people up.
- **One idea per message.** Long, winding messages are tiring to read in a second language.
- **Do not shout.** Typing in ALL CAPS or repeating words does not make anything clearer.

If you are using a translator too, our guide on [why a translated message sounds rude](/blog/why-your-translated-message-sounds-rude/) explains how to keep your tone friendly.

Here are a few examples of natural phrases and easier versions.

| Natural, but hard to decode | Easier to follow |
|---|---|
| I'm swamped, let's catch up later. | I'm very busy now. Let's talk later. |
| That's not my cup of tea. | I don't like that very much. |
| Break a leg tomorrow! | Good luck tomorrow! |
| Hang on a sec. | Please wait a moment. |
| It's a piece of cake. | It's very easy. |

The easy version is not worse. It is just clearer, and it keeps the warmth.

## Ask about their level, and what they want

Early in the chat, ask a simple question: "How long have you been learning?" It gives you a sense of their level without making it feel like a test. Then ask what they want from the conversation: "Would you like me to correct your mistakes, or should I just chat?"

Some people want every error pointed out. Others want to talk freely and be left alone. You cannot guess which is which, so ask once and remember the answer.

## Correct kindly, and not too often

Correction is the touchiest part. A few rules help.

1. **Ask first.** Without permission, corrections can feel like a red pen.
2. **Fix what blocks understanding.** If a mistake causes confusion, clear it up. If it is only a small grammar slip, let it go.
3. **Rephrase naturally.** If they write "Yesterday I go to the cinema," you can answer, "Oh, you went to the cinema yesterday! What did you see?" They get the right form without a lecture.
4. **One thing at a time.** Five corrections in one message is a wall. Pick the most useful.
5. **Praise something specific.** "That sentence was really clear" feels better and teaches more than a generic "Your English is great."

## Check understanding the kind way

When you want to know whether they followed you, avoid "Do you understand?" It can sound like you doubt them. Try "Does that make sense?" or "Is there anything I should explain differently?" It puts the responsibility on your explanation, not on their ability.

If they say they did not understand, do not repeat yourself louder. Rephrase with simpler words, or give an example.

## Be patient with time

They may reply slowly because they are looking up words or building a sentence, and a gap between messages is not a sign of lost interest. Using a second language is tiring, and many learners need breaks. Let them set the pace.

## Make it a two-way exchange

If you are both learning each other's languages, share the work. Write half in one language and half in the other, or take turns by day or by topic. When you are the learner, you will understand exactly how they feel. Our guide to [ten ways to practice a foreign language by chatting](/blog/ways-to-practise-a-foreign-language-by-chatting/) has ideas for that.

You can also learn a few words of theirs. Even a simple "thank you" in their language shows you are in this together. Try our [how to say thank you in 20 languages](/blog/how-to-say-thank-you-in-different-languages/).

## Voice messages: ask first

Voice notes are great practice for listening, but they can be hard if the speaker is fast or has a strong accent. Ask whether they would like them. If so, speak more slowly and clearly than usual, and send a short text version if you can.

## Keep it fun

A conversation partner is not a teacher. The goal is to talk, laugh and learn something about each other, with language growing as a side effect. If it starts to feel like a lesson, lighten it up. Ask about their day, their food, their weekend.

## Last thought

The best language partners are patient, curious and a little forgiving. If you remember what it feels like to try to say something in a language you barely know, you already know how to be one.
