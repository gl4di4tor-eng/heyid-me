It feels like [[hello]] has always been around. It hasn't. For most of history, English speakers greeted each other with phrases like "good day" or "how do you do". Hello, in its various spellings, was something you shouted to get someone's attention from across a field.

## Where the word came from

Older forms such as [[hallo]], [[holla]] and [[hullo]] were calls for attention, a bit like "hey, over here!" By the 1800s they were showing up in print, but nobody used them to greet a person standing in front of them. That would have sounded oddly loud.

## The telephone changed everything

When the telephone arrived in the 1870s, there was a real problem: nobody knew how to start a call. You couldn't see the other person, so a polite bow or handshake was no use. Alexander Graham Bell reportedly liked [[ahoy]], the sailor's call. Thomas Edison argued for hello, and Edison's version won.

Within a few decades hello was the standard way to answer a call. Telephone operators, who were mostly women, were even nicknamed "hello girls". From the phone it spread into everyday life, and people started using it face to face too.

## How other countries answer the phone

Not everyone landed on hello. A few that are worth knowing:

- [[Moshi moshi]] (Japanese): a popular explanation says it comes from a phrase meaning "I speak, I speak", a way of telling the caller that a real person is on the line.
- [[Pronto]] (Italian): it means "ready", as in "I'm ready, go ahead".
- [[Dígame]] (Spanish, mostly in Spain): "tell me". In Mexico people usually say [[bueno]], which means "good".
- [[Алло]] (Russian): close to the English hello, and used the same way.

## What it tells us

Greetings are younger and more flexible than they look. A word that began as a shout across a field became the world's most recognisable opening, mostly because a new gadget needed one. It's a reminder that nobody is born knowing the "right" way to start a conversation. Every greeting you use was invented by someone, at some point, to solve a small problem.
