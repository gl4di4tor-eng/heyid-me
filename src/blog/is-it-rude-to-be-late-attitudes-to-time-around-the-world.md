Imagine you've agreed to meet a friend at eight. At 8:10, you're checking your phone. At 8:20 you're annoyed. At 8:30 your friend arrives, relaxed and smiling, with no apology. Who's in the wrong?

Maybe nobody. Different cultures treat time differently, and what feels like basic politeness in one place can feel stiff in another.

## Two rough styles

The anthropologist Edward T. Hall described two broad approaches to time. In what he called monochronic cultures, people do one thing at a time, follow schedules closely and treat being late as a lack of respect. In polychronic cultures, people are more comfortable with several things happening at once, and relationships often matter more than the clock.

Think of these as tendencies, not rules. Every country has punctual people and relaxed ones, and a single person can be both depending on the situation.

## What this looks like in practice

- In Germany, Switzerland and Japan, arriving on time for a meeting is the baseline, and arriving early is common.
- In Spain, dinner often starts at nine or ten in the evening, and a party that begins "at eight" may not fill up until much later.
- In many parts of Latin America, the Middle East and Africa, social invitations are understood to be flexible. Arriving exactly on time to a private party can sometimes even catch the host unprepared.
- In Indonesia, people joke about [[jam karet]], "rubber time", a loose way of treating schedules.

Work and social settings are different, too. A country that is relaxed about parties can be strict about business meetings, and vice versa.

## Why you shouldn't take it personally

When someone is late by your standards, it usually says nothing about you. And when you're the one who arrives on the dot, you aren't being rude either. You're following a different set of unwritten rules.

The trouble starts when two people assume their own rules are universal. That's when "he doesn't respect me" or "she's so uptight" shows up in someone's head, even though no one meant anything by it.

## A simple fix for online chats

The easiest way around it is to ask. Plans made online across countries have extra moving parts, including time zones, so a bit of clarity helps.

- Say the time and the place: "8 pm Warsaw time", not "tonight".
- Ask how flexible it is: "Do you mean exactly 8, or around 8?"
- Agree what to do if someone is late: "If I'm not there by 8:15, I'll message you."

None of this has to feel formal. It's just a way of making sure both of you are on the same clock.
