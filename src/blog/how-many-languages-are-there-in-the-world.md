Ask people how many languages exist and you'll get guesses from a hundred to a few thousand. The real answer is bigger than most expect: somewhere above 7,000. The exact figure moves a little depending on who's counting, because the line between a language and a dialect is partly a political question, not just a linguistic one.

## A handful of languages carry most of the people

Here's the odd part. Those 7,000-plus languages are not spread evenly. About two dozen languages, including Mandarin, Spanish, English, Hindi, Arabic, Portuguese, Bengali and Russian, are spoken by more than half of the world's population. Everything else shares what's left.

That means many languages have very few speakers. Plenty have fewer than a thousand, and some are known to only a handful of elderly people in a single village.

## Where the variety is

Language diversity clusters in particular places. Papua New Guinea is the famous example, with over 800 languages in a country of around ten million people. Indonesia, Nigeria and India also have hundreds each. Mountains, islands and dense forests tend to produce more languages, because communities stay isolated for long stretches and drift apart in how they speak.

Compare that with Europe, where a much smaller number of languages cover a lot of ground, partly because of centuries of nation-building and standardised schooling.

## About four in ten are in trouble

Roughly 40 per cent of the world's languages are considered endangered, meaning fewer and fewer children are learning them. This usually isn't about anyone banning a language. It happens quietly, when parents decide their kids will have better chances in a bigger language and stop passing the smaller one on.

When a language fades, a lot goes with it: local names for plants and weather, ways of telling stories, jokes that only work one way. That's why linguists and communities work to record languages and teach them again.

## Why this matters for a chat

Most people reading this are used to meeting people who share a language. But anyone you meet online could speak any one of those thousands. Even the person who writes to you in English might have grown up speaking something completely different at home, and might be translating in their head as they go.

A small practical point follows from that. Keep your messages simple and clear, avoid idioms, and be patient with pauses. And if you're curious, ask. "What language did you grow up speaking?" is a surprisingly good question, and the answers are often more interesting than you'd guess.
