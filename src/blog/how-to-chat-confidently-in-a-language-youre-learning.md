You have been studying a language for months. You know the grammar, you have flashcards, you have finished a few lessons. And yet when a real person writes to you, you freeze.

That freeze is incredibly common, and it has very little to do with how much you know. It is about fear: of mistakes, of looking silly, of the other person getting bored. The good news is that this fear is easier to manage than you think. Here is how.

## Mistakes are how it works

People who speak a language well made thousands of mistakes on the way. A wrong word, a missing ending, a sentence that came out funny. That is not a failure of learning. That is learning.

It helps to remember what you feel when someone else makes mistakes in your language. Usually you do not think less of them. You think, "Good for them for trying." The person you are chatting with is almost certainly thinking the same.

## Tell them you are learning

This is the easiest tool you have. One simple sentence at the start sets the right expectations and takes pressure off both of you.

| Language | "I'm still learning." |
|---|---|
| Spanish | [[Todavía estoy aprendiendo.]] |
| French | [[J'apprends encore.]] |
| German | [[Ich lerne noch.]] |
| Italian | [[Sto ancora imparando.]] |
| Portuguese | [[Ainda estou aprendendo.]] |

You only need to say it once. Apologizing in every message makes the chat feel heavy. One clear line is enough.

## Prepare a small toolkit

You do not need to be fluent. You need a handful of phrases that rescue you when you are lost. Learn these first and keep them where you can copy them.

| Language | "Could you say that more slowly?" |
|---|---|
| Spanish | [[¿Puedes repetirlo más despacio?]] |
| French | [[Tu peux répéter plus lentement ?]] |
| German | [[Kannst du das bitte langsamer sagen?]] |
| Italian | [[Puoi ripetere più lentamente?]] |
| Portuguese | [[Pode repetir mais devagar?]] |

Add three more to your toolkit: "What does this word mean?", "How do you say this in your language?" and "Can you correct me?" Those three phrases turn almost any confusing moment into a lesson.

## Write first, then check

It is tempting to translate everything with a tool and send it. That is fine for hard words, but try writing the sentence yourself first, even if you think it is wrong. Then check it. You learn much more that way, and the other person gets to hear your real voice.

Use a translator like a dictionary, not like a ghostwriter. Beware of false friends too, which are words that look familiar but mean something else. Our guide to [false friends](/blog/false-friends-words-that-look-the-same-but-arent/) lists the classic ones.

## Start small and build a habit

Do not aim for long conversations at the beginning. Set a tiny goal, such as five messages a day. Short and regular beats long and rare. After a few weeks, five messages become ten, and the fear gets smaller.

Some easy first messages to use:

1. Say hello and tell them you are learning.
2. Ask what the weather is like.
3. Ask what they ate today.
4. Ask for one word in their language.
5. Say thank you.

If you run out of ideas, our guide on [how to keep a conversation going](/blog/how-to-keep-a-conversation-going/) has plenty.

## A tiny script for your first chat

Here is what a short, easy first exchange in Spanish can look like. You can copy the structure for almost any language.

- **You:** [[¡Hola! Estoy aprendiendo español. ¿Cómo estás?]] ("Hi! I'm learning Spanish. How are you?")
- **Them:** [[¡Muy bien! ¿De dónde eres?]] ("Very well! Where are you from?")
- **You:** [[Soy de Canadá. ¿Y tú?]] ("I'm from Canada. And you?")

Three messages, three tiny sentences, and you have a real conversation. Swap in your own country and language, and you are ready.

## What to do when you do not understand

It will happen constantly. Here is a calm way to deal with it.

- **Read it twice.** The first time, look for words you know. The second time, look for the rest.
- **Pick out the one word that blocks you.** You rarely need to understand every word. Look up one, not all.
- **Ask a specific question.** "What does this word mean?" is easier to answer than "I don't understand."
- **Guess and check.** "Do you mean that you are going to the beach?" is a great way to confirm.
- **Pause if you need to.** You can say you need a minute. Nobody minds.

## Keep a list of corrections

When someone corrects you, write it down. Keep a small note on your phone with the sentence, the correction and the date. In a month you will see how many things you now say correctly, and that feels wonderful. It is also real proof that the mistakes were useful.

## Voice messages when you are ready

Writing is the gentlest way to start, because you have time to think. When you feel more comfortable, try a short voice message. It feels scarier and teaches a lot about pronunciation. Our guide to [ten ways to practice a language by chatting](/blog/ways-to-practise-a-foreign-language-by-chatting/) explains how to build up to it.

## Last thought

Confidence does not come before you start. It comes from starting. Every conversation you survive, even a clumsy one, makes the next one easier. Send the message.
